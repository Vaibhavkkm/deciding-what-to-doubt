\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

An environmental monitoring network is a machine for producing doubt. A
single automatic weather station reporting eleven quantities at five minute
intervals generates over three thousand readings a day, and a modest national
network multiplies that by a hundred stations. Nobody looks at those numbers.
They flow into databases, feed flood forecasts and drought bulletins, anchor
long climate records, and calibrate the models that agricultural and water
authorities act on. Somewhere between the anemometer and the decision, a
judgement has to be made about every single value: keep it, flag it, or throw
it away. Since no human can make that judgement three thousand times a day
per station, the judgement is delegated to algorithms, and the design of
those algorithms turns out to be a surprisingly deep statistical problem with
a surprisingly long history.

I spent the past five months inside that problem. My Master's thesis, written
alongside this course, documents an internship at a Luxembourg research
institute where I extended and audited an automated quality control pipeline
for an eco-hydrological observatory: physical limit checks, seasonal
plausibility bands, stuck-sensor detection, spike detection with a robust
score, cross-parameter consistency rules, and comparisons against
neighbouring stations. Building such a system raises questions the code
itself never answers. Why a modified z-score and not a plain one? Where does
a threshold of exactly 3.5, or 30, or 300 come from, and who is accountable
for it? Why do operational systems still run rule batteries from the 1990s
when the anomaly detection literature has moved on to learned models? This
reading report is my attempt to answer those questions properly, by going
back to the works where the answers were first written down.

The reading list, assembled with my supervisor's blessing to follow my own
curiosity, spans 170 years of a single intellectual thread: how a measurement
earns distrust. \citet{grubbs1969} consolidated the classical statistics of
outlying observations into the tests that still carry his name.
\citet{iglewicz1993} repackaged the robust revolution of the 1970s into a
practitioner's rule, the modified z-score, that now sits inside more sensor
pipelines than its authors could have imagined. \citet{shafer2000} described
the first quality assurance system for a large automated surface network,
the Oklahoma Mesonet, and fixed the architecture that nearly every
operational network has copied since. \citet{campbell2013} generalised that
architecture into a framework for streaming ecological sensor data and gave
the field its slogan: quantity is nothing without quality. \citet{leigh2019}
brought the statisticians back in, formalising anomaly detection for in situ
water quality sensors and measuring, honestly, how well modern methods
actually perform. Read in sequence, the five works are less a pile of papers
than one long argument, passed between astronomers, ballisticians, quality
engineers, meteorologists, ecologists and statisticians, about what it means
for a number to be believable.

The report follows the five-role structure suggested for this course, with
each role applied across the whole reading list rather than one role per
paper. Section~\ref{sec:readinglist} presents the list itself and the logic
of its selection. Section~\ref{sec:historians} is the Historians' section: it
reconstructs the context in which each work was written, from a scandal over
discarded astronomical observations in the 1850s to a flash flood in Tulsa
that motivated a state-wide sensor network. Section~\ref{sec:technicians} is
the Technicians' section: it restates the central methods of all five works
in a single notation and isolates the anatomy they share.
Section~\ref{sec:experimentalists} is the Experimentalists' section: I
implement the methods in Python and reproduce their characteristic
behaviours, including their failure modes, on a synthetic benchmark with
known ground truth. Section~\ref{sec:futurists} is the Futurists' section: it
traces citation trajectories, standardisation and software descendants up to
the current frontier. Section~\ref{sec:critics} is the Critics' section: it
reads each work against the others and against my own operational
experience, and names what they leave unsaid.
Section~\ref{sec:synthesis} draws the threads together.

Two disclaimers belong up front. First, this report is not a literature
review for my thesis and does not describe the system I built; where my
internship experience sharpens a question, I say so in a sentence and move
on. Second, the experiments in Section~\ref{sec:experimentalists} use
synthetic data by design. I wanted ground truth, controlled fault injection
and exact reproducibility, three things no real archive provides, and the
price, which I discuss there, is that my quantitative results characterise
the methods on a model of reality rather than reality itself.
