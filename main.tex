\section*{Abstract}
Every automated quality control system for environmental sensor data answers
the same question thousands of times a day: is this reading to be trusted?
This report reads five works that shaped how that question is answered, from
Grubbs' consolidation of classical outlier tests, through the robust
reformulation of Iglewicz and Hoaglin, the first fully operational network
quality assurance system at the Oklahoma Mesonet, and the streaming QA/QC
framework of Campbell and colleagues, to the anomaly detection framework of
Leigh and colleagues for in situ water sensors. Following the five-role
format of the course, I trace where the ideas came from, restate the methods
in one notation, reproduce their central behaviours in Python on a controlled
synthetic benchmark, follow their influence to the current research frontier,
and critique them. Two threads organise the whole story. First, every method
in this lineage is a statistic plus a reference set plus a threshold, and
what changed over 170 years is where the reference set comes from: theory,
then history, then neighbouring sensors, then learned models. Second, the
thresholds have always been the weakest joint. My experiments show that the
textbook constant of the modified z-score does not transfer to autocorrelated
high-frequency data, and that no threshold alone can buy both full detection
and silence.

\section*{Relation to the Master's thesis}
My thesis describes the design, implementation and audit of an automated
quality control pipeline for the environmental monitoring network of a
Luxembourg research institute. This report deliberately does not describe
that system. It reads the literature the system quietly stands on, and it is
substantially different in aim, structure and content from the literature
review contained in the thesis: the thesis review surveys what exists in
order to position an implementation, while this report studies five works as
objects in their own right, historically, technically, experimentally and
critically.
